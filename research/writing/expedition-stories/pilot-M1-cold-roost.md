### 0.5 The tier-5 material tag vs. the safe-hatching outcome (owner question)

The tension, stated plainly: the game's dispatch labels the choice "Material to gather." A gather
reads like cargo — something taken. This pilot's shared outcome (S2, fixed since the plan, not to
be changed without the owner's decision) is that **every egg hatches safe.** Cargo does not hatch;
a hatched egg was not gathered. Prose that narrated the chosen egg as collected cargo would
contradict S2, and S2 is why the three tellings can coexist at all (it is what keeps the crag's
story true whichever route was viewed).

Proposed story-compatible presentation (branch focus, not cargo manifest — no gameplay rule
invented): the material tag names **which egg's situation defines the telling**. The chosen egg is
the event's subject, not its freight, and the prose never shows an egg being taken from an active
nest. What the expedition pays out is a game-economy matter and, per the settlement code checked
for this pilot (source/core.js:269), awards stamps, likeability, boosts and possible bonuses —
**no ingredient, and no egg**. So prose must neither claim an egg is handed over at settlement nor
dress up a substitute. Shed feathers or down may flavor a telling as the roost's own parting gift,
but are never presented as the selected tier-5 egg or as the gather award. The "Material to
gather" wording itself is a separate wording correction (owner-assigned; outside narrative scope)
once this interpretation is confirmed. The pilot's prose already complies — no telling narrates
egg collection or an egg award.

If the owner instead wants the egg literally gathered as an item, S2 must be revised at plan level
(e.g., the branch focus egg is collected/relocated rather than kept) — that is a fixed-outcome
change, raised for decision before any batch, and the pilot does not assume it.

## 2. Route 1 — Life · `Rainbow bird's egg`

**The telling.** The frost did one useful thing: it knocked the rainbow clutch free of its high
nest before anyone had noticed it sat light there. By the time the five reached the crag, the
strange eggs lay cooling among ordinary ones, bright as sugar. The crisis was not cold alone — it
was custody. Every nest on the crag belonged to somebody, and wild things do not accept strange
eggs on the strength of a speech about community.

Resolution ran on two tools. Daisy bricked the ledge in oven-warmed straw — heat only, tested
first against her own cheek like checking proof — and turned each strange egg a quarter-twist
twice a day, the way she'd steady dough through its rise. Neet made the introductions: a feather
from each host nest carried under her nose and back, morning by morning, until the crag's birds
scented the strangers as tenants rather than trespassers. By the third morning, the crag had
stopped noticing which eggs were strange.

It was the first evening — testing how much weight the crag's stone would hold — that Dolbur
hauled himself up a rope, dropped the coil back down for Kyle, and Kyle caught it one-handed.
"An introduction," Dolbur called it. Kyle, more precise, called it "a catch." They have opened
with it ever since.

The frost, by Claire's log, broke inside the week and left the orchard untouched. And once the
hatching was behind them, the crag was named and protected then and there: the patrol schedule
went up in Claire's handwriting on the spot, which is how the site held onto everything that
happened there.

**Outcome (this telling's, distinct):** the rainbow chicks hatched into borrowed warmth and fledged
all over the crag — among bluebirds, among the wyvern family's edges, everywhere — until the whole
roost considered itself mixed. Every spring since, the fledglings' rainbow feathers turn up
catching light along the orchard trellis, and the town walks out to see them. The bluebird and
wyvern eggs hatched without incident that same week, and Neet entered the whole roost's hatch-day
in the shrine almanac that evening — one line, and the first page of the three-version record the
almanac keeps. The crag's protection, in this telling, is famous mostly for beauty.

### 2.1 Perspective — Daisy (baker), Life route

> Daisy never once got upset, not even the morning her first straw blanket went out too thick and
> insulated worse. "That is all right. We can try again another time," she said — and she
> meant it, which is the only sentence she owns that nobody can improve.
>
> What she told no one at the ledge: the strange eggs had been warm at the center when she found
> them, like dough that had gotten one more proof than anyone knew. So the rainbow mother had left
> recently — which is why she never once panicked about the warmth schedule. What she also
> kept quiet: her two quarter-turns a day, borrowed from proofing, stitched to a label she wrote so
> Neet would not worry about which egg had been turned. Eggs stay sealed and apart from anything
> that goes in an oven, of course. The straw is a heat trick, and the heat is for the eggs.

*Adds (not retold in the main telling): the eggs were still warm at the center — the mother
left recently; the quarter-turn schedule with a written label; her first blanket failed and her
documented "that is all right" floor line. Agrees with the spine: all eggs hatch safe.*

### 2.2 Perspective — Claire (knight), Life route

> The schedule that hangs on the crag now is in her handwriting, and she says so plainly, without
> flourish: she filed the protection reclassification before anyone asked, because paperwork
> delayed is patrolling gone. The route is hers — two boundary walks to the orchard fence, one at
> dawn, one at dusk, three on frost nights when the midday cold-check is added — and the standard
> two are timed to the frost's retreat rather than the clock,
> which is how the frost gets its date: it broke within the week, on her log.
>
> What she does not say on the record: she watched Neet carry feathers nose-to-nose to every nest
> like a diplomat presenting credentials, and it struck her that the shrine's machinery of
> welcome was doing more for the roost than the schedule ever would. The regulation, in her
> opinion, exists to hold the door; it does not have to be the reason anyone enters.

*Adds (not retold): the filing-before-asked detail behind the schedule the telling now records
(her private angle on spine fact S3); the frost's exact
break, dated; her private observation that welcomes do the work. Agrees with: the spine, Neet's
introductions, Daisy's steady warmth (she saw the turned labels).*

## 3. Route 2 — Mana · `Bluebird's egg`

**The telling.** The bluebird clutch dulled overnight — shells gone from paint-bright to wet
chalk, and the song inside failing with them. Not an enemy, not a tangle, not a wind. A song
losing its note. Dolbur offered loudly to fight the frost; he was politely assigned elsewhere.

The fix took patience and one heresy. Neet led with the shrine's lullabies; the nest flatly
refused each correct one. So she did the thing clerics are not trained for: she sang the lullaby
wrong — deliberately, flat where the song rises — until the note inside matched her error and
held it. The shells brightened within two nights. The rest was quiet logistics: Kyle wrapped the
ladder's metal rungs in cloth so nothing rang, then held stillness for hours at a stretch while
the rite ran, and Daisy restocked the leaning ledges with oven-warmed straw — heat only — so the
other two clutches rode warm all the while. On the coldest night, Kyle's ladder-foot slid on the
icy rock below; it was Dolbur, out on the high path taking the frost-line reading, who dropped
his spare rope down for him. Kyle caught it mid-slip. Both called it an introduction; neither
called it a rescue.

The frost broke within the week, as it does in every telling; this telling's only slow grief is
that the song did not break with it. When the hatch came, Neet recorded the hatch-day in the
shrine almanac — one line among the three versions the almanac keeps — and once the quiet could
be trusted to hold, Claire added the crag to her standing patrol schedule, in her own handwriting:
the site that asked for hush was going to get a watch.

**Outcome (this telling's, distinct):** the chick hatched healthy but sang flat all spring — the
crag's mornings went silent except for one patient bluebird — and its true song came back only
after winter, which the region remembers as the quiet season. The rainbow and wyvern eggs hatched
unhurried and unharmed that same spring; in this telling their keeping is one quiet line each.
Neet's wrong-lullaby rite is entered
in the shrine's records as a working rite (route mark), and the town learned to walk the orchard
path hushed at nesting time, a custom that stayed.

### 3.1 Perspective — Neet (cleric), Mana route

> Neet will confirm, formally and without embarrassment, that the difficult part was not the rite.
> The difficult part was learning to sing badly. Correct lullabies come easy to a steward; the
> wrong one required her to practice — at midnight, in the shrine pantry, where her voice carries
> least — until the flat note sat where she wanted it without her hating it.
>
> What her written rite omits, and what she would tell the shopkeeper if asked: the chick did not
> improve when she sang truthfully. It sat through three correct verses and dulled further, and it
> was the mock version it finally matched. Her private note in the margin says: *some things
> mend when you stop correcting them.* The almanac gets the hatch-day; the rite record gets the
> method; what neither gets is that her midnight practice kept the bakery awake — they were kind
> about it, and she has been polite about not knowing they heard.

*Adds (not retold): the midnight practice; the margin note; her failure-first sequence (correct
songs failed the nest before wrongness worked). Agrees with: the spine (hatch-day in the
almanac); Claire's silence (the pantry and the tree line both stayed quiet).*

### 3.2 Perspective — Kyle (traveler), Mana route

> Kyle counts a dare in challenges mastered, and this one fought dirty: it was stillness, and
> stillness cannot be trained on any rock he knows. His solution was inventory thinking. He knew
> the ladder's metal rungs rang — he had heard them ring half the climb up — so he wrapped them in
> cloth strips from his kit (his spare-rope shelf also donated the strips; his storeroom has a
> label for everything and now, apparently, clothing).
>
> What he does not say at the guild: he counted the shifts by the number of times the chick went
> quiet — seventeen, over the two nights — and he was prouder of the silence that came when his
> count ended, because it meant the hush held without him. He checked the wind once every
> hour, upwind for sound, because in his experience the thing that breaks a still vigil is never
> the person holding it; it is whatever the wind was carrying.

*Adds (not retold): the cloth-rung trick; the count of seventeen silences; the wind-check habit.
Agrees with: the two-night brightening; Neet's rite timing; the frost's break within the week.*

## 4. Route 3 — Stamina · `Wyvern's egg`

**The telling.** The wyvern mother came down circling — wide, patient, and in no mood for
committee — while her egg rolled loose along the failing ledge's lip. Wild things do not yield
custody; the move had to be offered, not taken. Claire's cordons closed the orchard path and every
roofline a spectator might use; the region's people, to their credit, stayed behind them.

Dolbur climbed. The rock was winter-hardened and so was his thinking, and one piton bent on the
first reach — he reports this, immediately and cheerfully, which is half of why the climb worked.
Kyle anchored him from the storeroom's spare rope and counted every carry. But the move had to be
offered, not taken, and its hour belonged to someone else: Daisy had been up all night regardless,
and what she carried out at first light settled the matter better than any argument could. The
loaf went onto the warm stone at the quarry's foot; the mother circled once more, landed, and the
landing decided it. Four carries later — dry stone, no blasting in its history, room to turn
around — the brood was whole at the old quarry ledge, and the mother's next circle was around them.

The rest held to the same account: the carry baskets were lined with oven-warmed straw — heat
only; the trick that would afterward be called the roost trick earned its second career as
courier-bedding — and the frost, true to every version of that week, broke inside seven days and
left the orchard untouched. When the gear came down, the last coil dropped from the ledge straight
into Kyle's waiting hands. Neet entered the roost's hatch-days into the shrine almanac as they
came, one line beside the red and blue entries, and as Claire's cordons came down they came down
into something:
the crag went onto the patrol schedule in her handwriting, and the quarry ledge went onto the map.

**Outcome (this telling's, distinct):** the crag lost a tenant and gained a neighbor. The wyvern
household lives at the quarry ledge now — a fixed point on the region's map, on the adjusted patrol
route — and Dolbur is, as far as anyone can tell, the one person the mother tolerates. The
bluebird and rainbow clutches hatched untroubled in this telling — their crises are the other
tellings' stories — and the moved wyvern egg hatched safe at the quarry: the first brood its new
household ever raised.

### 4.1 Perspective — Dolbur (warrior), Stamina route

> Dolbur's report of the climb is his proudest document, and its first line is an apology: *one
> (1) piton, bent, reported immediately.* He bent it reaching past the frost line, told Kyle
> before his boots were down, and re-roped. That part he tells everyone. What he does not tell:
> he heard the mother before anyone else — a sound like stone getting annoyed — and he started
> the climb early because of it, so the climb was already rigged and half-roped when the crew
> finished arguing about who would climb.
>
> He also got the carry count wrong — announced two, it became four — and corrected himself at the
> guild at the top of his voice, which is how the whole town knows both numbers. His private
> discovery of the whole crawl is smaller: he is the only one the mother circles with, instead of
> at. He has decided this is a rank, and his official position is that he holds it proudly.

*Adds (not retold): the bent piton, reported rather than hidden (arc milestone — his documented
mistake used as growth, not erased); the early start because he heard her first; the wrong carry
count, cheerfully corrected. Agrees with: the move completed before noon; Claire's cordons; Kyle's
anchor and carries.*

### 4.2 Perspective — Daisy (baker), Stamina route

> The story people tell about the dawn loaf is that the mother simply accepted the move because
> Daisy, absurdly, baked. That is true and also not — she baked because she could not help it; a
> long watch with no meal service is, in her opinion, a form of cold. She built the fire at the
> back of the bakery, not the orchard front, so no heat or noise reached the eggs, and the loaf
> never crossed the cordon toward the eggs — it went the other way entirely, out to the quarry.
>
> What she has not said before: the mother was not circling the egg. She was circling the quarry —
> Daisy watched from her window all night the way she watches proof, rise measured by how much
> shape changes. When the loaf went out onto the warm stone at dawn, the mother landed, and the
> landing decided it. Four carries later — Kyle counted, Daisy baked — the brood was home, and
> every loaf she sent up after that was a housewarming.

*Adds (not retold): the quarried circling (the mother was inspecting the future home, not guarding
the old egg); the fire built at the bakery's back; the loaf's route — out to the quarry, never
toward the eggs. Agrees with: the dawn timing; the brood's move; the frost breaking that week.*
